How Camera Works

Understanding How Cameras Capture Images: Exploring the optical pathways, physical components, exposure mechanics, and sensor sensitivities of Still, DSLR, and Mirrorless systems.

Understanding How Camera Captures Images: Parts of the Camera

Whether working with a traditional film camera, a Digital Single-Lens Reflex (DSLR), or a modern mirrorless system, cameras capture photons and focus them onto a light-sensitive recording medium through synchronized optical and electronic operations.

The Camera Lens & Focal Length

Core Optical Hardware

Lens Anatomy & Controls

  • Aperture / F-Stop / Iris: Mechanical diaphragm opening regulating light volume and depth of field.
  • Focal Length: Defines angle of view (Wide, Standard, Telephoto, or Zoom).
  • Focus: Aligning lens elements to resolve subjects cleanly on the sensor.
  • Neutral Density (ND) Filters: Optical filters placed in front of the lens to cut light. Professional video cameras feature them built-in on a rotating internal wheel.
  • Shutter & Shutter Speed: Physical or electronic gate timing the duration of exposure.
  • Image Sensor (Film): The photosensitive silicon plane (CMOS/CCD) or celluloid film stock.
  • Sensitivity / Gain / ISO: Electronic signal amplification of the sensor.
  • White Balance: Color temperature compensation ensuring neutral whites across lighting conditions.
  • Viewfinder / Display: Film movie cameras and DSLRs utilize optical viewfinders with reflexive mirrors and pentaprisms; digital cinema cameras, mirrorless bodies, and video cameras use digital electronic viewfinders (EVFs) and LCD monitor displays.
Optics & Perspective

Focal Length

  • Lenses come in various focal lengths, ranging from Ultra Wide-Angle (fisheye) to Super Telephoto (telescope).
  • Two Primary Optical Types:
    • Zoom Lenses: Variable focal length allowing changes in framing without moving the camera base.
    • Prime Lenses: Fixed focal length offering superior optical sharpness and wider maximum apertures.
  • Focal length is measured in millimeters (mm). The smaller the number (e.g., 18mm), the wider the angle of view; the larger the number (e.g., 200mm), the narrower the angle of view (telephoto).
  • Visual Impact on Perspective: Focal length plays as strong a role in creating visual media as lighting does — maybe even more so. If you want to shoot a person in a Medium Shot (from the stomach up), you could place the camera very close with a wide lens, or step 20 feet back and zoom in with a telephoto lens. While the size of the subject in the frame remains identical, everything else changes — particularly the subject's relationship to the background environment and spatial depth compression.

Light & Aperture (The Exposure Triangle)

Managing Incident Light & The Iris

Of course, the most obvious way to alter exposure is to add or remove light from the environment. This can be achieved through natural illumination (filming in direct sunlight, in open shade, or under overcast skies) or with dedicated artificial lighting instruments (utilizing bigger, brighter lights or dimming fixtures).

Aperture Mechanics: The aperture is a mechanical iris inside the lens housing that restricts or widens the beam of incoming light, functioning much like a water faucet handle controlling the volume of water traveling through a pipe.

  • Small Aperture (High f-stop number, e.g., f/16, f/22): Less light reaches the sensor, resulting in a large / deep depth of field where both foreground and background stay in focus.
  • Large Aperture (Low f-stop number, e.g., f/1.4, f/2.8): More light enters the lens, creating a shallow depth of field where the subject is sharp against a soft, blurred background.
  • Terminology: Also frequently referred to as the Iris or f-stop.
  • f-Stop Scale: The numerical value defining the opening diameter relative to focal length. A smaller number denotes a wider physical aperture.
  • Maximum Aperture: The widest physical opening (smallest f-number) is dictated by the optical engineering and physical build of the lens.

Sensor Sensitivity: Gain & ISO

Exposure sensitivity determines how responsive the recording medium is to photons hitting the sensor surface. Video engineering and photography use different measurement systems for this property:

Broadcast & Video Production

Gain (in Decibels - dB)

Video cameras measure sensor sensitivity amplification as Gain in decibels (dB), baseline starting at 0 dB and increasing in steps of +3 dB to double the electronic sensitivity (+3dB, +6dB, +9dB, +12dB, etc.).

Still & Cinema Cameras

ISO Rating

DSLR, mirrorless, and digital cinema cameras use the ISO scale (standardized from traditional ASA film speed), with native baseline sensitivity usually set at ISO 100 or ISO 200.

The Gain / ISO Trade-Off

  • Sensor Sensitivity: Defines the responsiveness of the digital image sensor (or film emulsion speed) to collecting light.
  • High Gain / High ISO The higher the Gain or ISO value, the more sensitive the sensor becomes, enabling you to shoot in dim environments. Trade-Off: The image becomes noisier (or displays heavy grain in film), reducing dynamic range, color saturation, and fine detail.
  • Low Gain / Low ISO The lower the Gain or ISO setting (e.g., ISO 100, 0 dB), the cleaner, crisper, and more defined the image. Trade-Off: The sensor is less light-sensitive, requiring stronger ambient light or wide apertures for proper exposure.

Shutter Speed, Frame Rate & Neutral Density (ND) Filters

Controlling the duration of sensor exposure and managing sunlight through optical density filters:

Shutter Speed & Frame Rate (FPS)

The longer the sensor is exposed to light, the more light it collects. This happens within fractions of a second.

Frame Rate Limits: If shooting video at 30 fps (Frames Per Second), each frame can only be exposed for a maximum of 1/30 of a second or less.

The 180-Degree Rule In standard cinematography, shutter speed is set to double the frame rate (e.g., 24 fps at 1/48s or 1/50s; 30 fps at 1/60s) to produce natural motion blur that matches human vision. Faster shutter speeds (e.g., 1/500s) eliminate motion blur and make fast action look crisp and staccato.

Neutral Density (ND) Filters

Neutral Density filters function as "sunglasses for your camera." When shooting outdoors in bright direct sunlight, you often want a wide aperture (e.g., f/2.0) to blur the background while locking the video shutter speed at 1/50s. Without an ND filter, the image would be severely overexposed and washed out.

ND filters evenly reduce the intensity of all wavelengths of incoming light without altering color balance or hue, giving you complete artistic control over shutter speed and depth of field in broad daylight.
